# The Seven Essential Questions

*Quick Reference from The Coaching Habit by Michael Bungay Stanier*

---

## Overview

These seven questions help mentors stay curious longer and resist the "Advice Monster." Use them in sequence or selectively based on the conversation.

---

## 1. The Kickstart Question

**"What's on your mind?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| Opening every conversation | Opens the door without leading. Lets the mentee set the agenda. |

**Tips:**
- Stay silent after asking. Let them fill the space.
- Don't suggest topics or prompt with "Is it about X?"

**Follow-up if they're vague:** "What's the most important thing we should talk about today?"

---

## 2. The AWE Question

**"And what else?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| After any answer to go deeper | The first answer is rarely the only answer or the best answer. Creates space for more. |

**Tips:**
- Ask it at least three times per topic
- Tone matters: curious, not interrogating
- Often the real issue emerges on the third "And what else?"

**When they say "Nothing else":** Move to the next question. Don't force it.

---

## 3. The Focus Question

**"What is the real challenge here for you?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| When the conversation feels scattered or unfocused | Cuts through symptoms to the root cause. The "for you" makes it personal, not abstract. |

**Tips:**
- Emphasize "for YOU" - not the team, not the company
- Helps shift from venting to ownership
- Often reveals the mentee already knows the answer

**If they deflect to others:** Gently redirect: "I hear that [others] are involved, but what's the challenge for you specifically?"

---

## 4. The Foundation Question

**"What do you want?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| When clarifying goals or before problem-solving | People often don't know what they actually want. This question forces clarity. |

**Tips:**
- Accept the first answer, then probe: "What else do you want?"
- Help distinguish wants vs. needs
- This may take time - don't rush

**Common response types:**
- Project outcomes (deliverables, results)
- Recognition (acknowledgment, visibility)
- Growth (skills, experience)
- Clarity (direction, decisions)
- Support (resources, help)

---

## 5. The Lazy Question

**"How can I help?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| Before offering any advice or assistance | Prevents you from solving the wrong problem. Puts the mentee in control of asking. |

**Tips:**
- Resist jumping in with solutions
- Let them be specific about what they need
- "What would be most helpful?" is a good variant

**If they say "I don't know":** Try: "What would you like me to do? Share experience? Make an introduction? Just listen?"

---

## 6. The Strategic Question

**"If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| When discussing new commitments, opportunities, or priorities | Every yes has a hidden no. Helps with prioritization and trade-offs. |

**Tips:**
- Use when mentee is overcommitted or adding new goals
- Helps surface opportunity cost
- Good for career decisions

**Follow-up:** "What will you stop doing, do less of, or delegate to make room for this?"

---

## 7. The Learning Question

**"What was most useful for you?"**

| When to Use | Why It Works |
|-------------|--------------|
| End of every conversation | Cements learning. Gives you feedback. Creates a positive close. |

**Tips:**
- Always end with this question
- Accept whatever they say - don't correct or add
- Their answer often reveals what mattered most

**If they struggle:** "What's one thing you'll take away from our conversation?"

## The Advice Monster Warning

Before giving advice, ask yourself:

- [ ] Have I asked at least three questions first?
- [ ] Do I truly understand the real challenge?
- [ ] Did they specifically ask for my advice?
- [ ] Is my advice based on their situation or my experience?

**Remember:** Your job is to help them think, not to think for them.

---

## Conversation Flow Example

```
Mentor: "What's on your mind?"
Mentee: "I'm struggling with my project deadline."
Mentor: "And what else?"
Mentee: "Well, I'm also not sure if I'm even working on the right things."
Mentor: "And what else?"
Mentee: "I guess I'm worried about how my manager perceives my performance."
Mentor: "What is the real challenge here for you?"
Mentee: "I think... I don't have clarity on priorities, and I'm afraid to ask."
Mentor: "What do you want?"
Mentee: "I want to feel confident I'm working on the most important things."
Mentor: "How can I help?"
Mentee: "Could you help me think through how to have that conversation with my manager?"
[Discussion happens]
Mentor: "What was most useful for you?"
Mentee: "Realizing I need to ask for clarity, not just assume."
```
